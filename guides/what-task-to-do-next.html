How to decide what task to do next

Choosing is often harder than doing. Here are five proven prioritization methods, a simple rule you can use in ten seconds, and how to hand the decision to an app.

Last updated: November 17, 2026

Short answer

Do the task with the nearest real deadline that also matters. If nothing is due, do the most important task you've been avoiding, first thing. If you're stuck between two, pick the one that unblocks other work. Or let an app like Taven weigh deadlines, importance and recurrence and pick for you.

The 10-second rule

When you just need an answer, go down this list and stop at the first “yes”:

  1. Is something due today (or overdue) with real consequences? Do that.
  2. Is something blocking other people or other tasks? Do that.
  3. Is there an important task you've been avoiding? Do that — now, before energy runs out.
  4. Otherwise, do the task that moves your most important goal forward.

Five proven prioritization methods

1. The Eisenhower matrix

Sort tasks by two questions: is it urgent, and is it important? Urgent and important: do now. Important but not urgent: schedule. Urgent but not important: delegate or batch. Neither: drop. Its power is the second box — the important work that never feels urgent until it's a crisis.

2. Eat the frog

From Brian Tracy: do your biggest, most dreaded task first thing in the morning. Everything after feels easier, and procrastination doesn't get a chance to build.

3. The 1-3-5 rule

Each day, plan one big task, three medium tasks and five small ones. It forces realistic days and makes the priority obvious: the one big task.

4. Most Important Task (MIT)

Each evening, pick one to three tasks that would make tomorrow a success no matter what else happens. Do them before checking email or messages.

5. ABCDE method

Label every task: A = must do (serious consequences), B = should do, C = nice to do, D = delegate, E = eliminate. Never start a B while an A is open.

Common mistakes

  • Doing quick tasks to feel productive. Clearing ten trivial items feels good but leaves the important one untouched.
  • Re-prioritizing all day. Decide once, then commit until the task is done or something truly urgent appears.
  • Treating every task as high priority. If everything is a priority, nothing is.
  • Ignoring recurring commitments. Habits and routine tasks quietly compete with projects; account for them.

Frequently asked questions

How do I decide which task to do first?

Start with anything due today that has real consequences, then anything blocking others, then the important task you've been avoiding. If none apply, work on whatever moves your most important goal forward.

What is the best prioritization method?

The Eisenhower matrix is the most widely used and is good for sorting a long list. Eat the frog and the Most Important Task method are better for daily focus. Many people combine them.

Is there an app that decides what I should do next?

Taven for iPhone and iPad uses on-device AI to rank your tasks by importance, due date and recurrence, then highlights the single task you should do next and explains why.
