How to decide what task to do next

Choosing what to work on is often harder than the work itself. Here are five methods people have relied on for years, a rule you can use in about ten seconds, and what to do if you'd rather not decide at all.

Last updated: November 17, 2026

Short answer

Do the task with the nearest real deadline that also actually matters. If nothing's due, do the important task you've been avoiding, and do it first. If you're stuck between two, pick the one that unblocks other work. Or let an app like Taven look at your deadlines, priorities and repeating tasks and pick for you.

The ten-second rule

When you just need an answer right now, run down this list and stop at the first yes.

  1. Is anything due today (or already overdue) where missing it would actually cause problems? Do that.
  2. Is anything holding up other people, or blocking your other tasks? Do that.
  3. Is there an important task you've been dodging? Do that, now, while you still have the energy.
  4. If none of the above, do whatever moves your most important goal forward.

Five methods worth knowing

1. The Eisenhower matrix

Sort your tasks using two questions: is it urgent, and is it important? Urgent and important things get done now. Important but not urgent things get scheduled. Urgent but unimportant things get handed off or batched together. Anything that's neither gets dropped. The real value is that second group, the important work that never feels urgent until suddenly it's an emergency.

2. Eat the frog

This one was popularized by Brian Tracy. Do your biggest, most dreaded task first thing in the morning. Everything after that feels easier by comparison, and procrastination never gets a chance to build up.

3. The 1-3-5 rule

Each day, plan one big task, three medium ones and five small ones. It forces you into realistic days, and it makes your priority obvious. It's the big one.

4. Your Most Important Task

The night before, pick one to three tasks that would make tomorrow a good day no matter what else happens. Then do them before you open your email or your messages.

5. The ABCDE method

Give every task a letter. A means you must do it (there are real consequences). B means you should. C means it'd be nice. D means delegate it, and E means get rid of it. The only rule is that you never start a B while an A is still open.

A few traps to watch out for

The most common one is doing the quick little tasks so you feel productive. Clearing ten tiny things feels great, but the important thing is still sitting there afterwards.

Another is re-prioritizing all day long. Decide once, then stick with it until the task is finished or something genuinely urgent shows up. It's also easy to treat everything as high priority, which is the same as having no priorities at all. And don't forget your recurring commitments. Habits and routine tasks quietly compete with your projects for the same hours, so count them.

Frequently asked questions

How do I decide which task to do first?

Start with anything due today that has real consequences, then anything that's holding other people up, then the important task you've been avoiding. If none of those apply, work on whatever moves your biggest goal forward.

What's the best way to prioritize?

The Eisenhower matrix is the most popular, and it's great for sorting a long list. Eat the frog and the Most Important Task method are better for staying focused day to day. Plenty of people mix and match.

Is there an app that decides what I should do next?

Yes. Taven for iPhone and iPad uses on-device AI to rank your tasks by importance, due date and how often they repeat, then highlights the one task you should do next and tells you why.
